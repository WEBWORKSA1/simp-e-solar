Shopping for solar means hearing a lot of strange words. Mono, poly, thin-film, TOPCon, bifacial. Installers toss them around as if you already know what they mean. The good news is that the choice is simpler than it sounds. For most homes in the US today, one type has clearly won.

This guide explains the three main panel types, the newer cell designs inside modern panels, and which numbers on a spec sheet actually matter.

What are the three main types of solar panels?

All three types turn sunlight into electricity. They differ in how the light-catching material is made, which affects efficiency, looks and cost. If you are new to how cells work, start with our guide on how solar panels work.

Monocrystalline

These panels use cells cut from a single, pure crystal of silicon. The pure structure lets electrons move easily, which makes these panels the most efficient. They usually look solid black. Mono panels now make up the vast majority of new panels sold worldwide, according to the IEA.

Polycrystalline

These cells are made from many silicon fragments melted together. They look blue with a speckled, shattered-glass pattern. They were cheaper for years, but they are less efficient. As mono prices fell, poly mostly disappeared from the home market. You may still see them on older roofs or in some budget products.

Thin-film

Thin-film panels use a very thin layer of material, such as cadmium telluride (CdTe) or amorphous silicon, spread on glass or a flexible backing. They handle heat well and can be light or bendable. But they are much less efficient, so you need far more roof space for the same power. You mostly see them on large solar farms, RVs and some portable gear.

What are TOPCon, HJT, back-contact and bifacial panels?

These are not separate panel types. They are newer ways of building monocrystalline cells to squeeze out more power. You will see these words on spec sheets more and more.

PERC

PERC adds a reflective layer on the back of the cell. Light that passes through gets bounced back for a second chance. PERC was the standard for most of the last decade and is now being replaced.

TOPCon

TOPCon adds an ultra-thin layer on the back that helps stop electrons from getting lost before they are collected. In plain words, fewer electrons go to waste. TOPCon panels typically reach 22% to 23% efficiency and have become the new mainstream choice.

HJT (heterojunction)

HJT sandwiches a crystal silicon cell between thin layers of a different kind of silicon. It works especially well in hot weather and loses power more slowly over time. HJT panels often reach 22% to 24% efficiency but usually cost a bit more.

Back-contact

Normal cells have thin metal lines on the front that block a little light. Back-contact cells move all the wiring to the back. The front catches more light, and the panel looks clean and all-black. These are among the most efficient panels you can buy, often 23% to 24%, and also among the priciest.

Bifacial

Bifacial panels can collect light on both the front and back. The back catches light reflected off the ground. They shine on ground mounts over light surfaces, like white gravel or snow, where they can gain 5% to 20% more power. On a typical sloped roof, the back side sees little light, so the gain is small.

Don't get stuck on cell tech names. A quality panel with a strong warranty and a fair price matters more than whether it says TOPCon or HJT. Compare price per watt and warranty terms first.

How many watts does a solar panel make today?

Most residential panels sold today are rated between 400 and 460 watts. Ten years ago, 250 to 300 watts was normal. The panels did not get much bigger. They got more efficient.

The rated wattage is measured in lab conditions. On your roof, a 430 W panel in a place with 5 peak sun hours makes about 430 × 5 × 0.8 = 1,720 watt-hours, or about 1.7 kWh per day. Over a year, that is roughly 630 kWh from one panel.

Higher-wattage panels let you fit more power on a small roof. They do not make each kilowatt cheaper by default. What matters is the total system price divided by total watts. To see how many panels your home needs, try the system size calculator.

How fast do solar panels lose power?

All panels slowly lose output as they age. This is called degradation. Good modern mono panels lose about 0.25% to 0.5% per year after a slightly larger drop in the first year.

Here is the math. Say a panel loses 2% in year one, then 0.4% each year after:

  • After year 1: 98% of original output.
  • Years 2 to 25: 24 more years × 0.4% = 9.6% more loss.
  • After 25 years: about 98% − 9.6% = 88.4% of original output.

So a 430 W panel would still make about 380 W after 25 years. A system making 10,000 kWh in year one would still make about 8,800 kWh in year 25.

What warranties should solar panels have?

Panels come with two separate warranties. Read both.

  • Product warranty: Covers defects in materials and build quality. Older panels offered 10 to 12 years. Many quality panels now offer 25 years.
  • Performance (power) warranty: Promises the panel will still make a certain share of its rated power after a set time. A common promise today is 87% to 92% after 25 or 30 years.

A warranty is only as good as the company behind it. A 30-year promise from a maker that might not exist in five years is worth less than a 25-year promise from a stable one. Your installer's workmanship warranty is a third, separate protection. Learn more in our guide to choosing an installer.

Which solar panel type should you choose?

For a home roof, the answer is almost always monocrystalline. Here is a quick way to decide:

  • Normal roof, normal budget: Mono TOPCon panels in the 400 to 450 W range.
  • Small or crowded roof: The highest-efficiency mono you can afford, such as back-contact or HJT.
  • Very hot climate: Look for a low temperature coefficient (around −0.30%/°C or better). HJT often does well here.
  • Ground mount over a light surface: Bifacial panels can add real output.
  • RV, boat or curved surface: Flexible thin-film or lightweight mono.

Frequently asked questions

Are black solar panels better than blue ones?

Black usually means monocrystalline, which is more efficient than blue polycrystalline. All-black panels with black frames and backsheets may cost slightly more just for looks.

Is a higher-efficiency panel always worth it?

No. Efficiency matters most when roof space is tight. If you have plenty of room, cheaper standard panels can deliver the same total power at a lower price per watt.

What is a temperature coefficient?

It shows how much power a panel loses as it heats up. A value of −0.30%/°C means the panel loses 0.3% of its power for each degree Celsius above 25°C. Lower numbers are better.

Can I mix different panel types in one system?

It is possible with microinverters, but mixing panels on one string inverter can lower output. Most installers use one panel model per system to keep things simple.
