Most solar advice assumes you own a house with a sunny roof. If you rent an apartment, a condo or a house, that advice does not fit. You cannot bolt 20 panels onto a roof you do not own, and you might move in a year or two.

The good news is that you still have real choices. Some cut your power bill a little. Some just let you use sunlight for your own gear. A few cost nothing to try. This guide walks through each one, shows the rough numbers and helps you pick a starting point.

What is community solar and how does a renter join?

Community solar is a large solar farm somewhere in your area that many people share. You subscribe to a slice of its output. The farm sends power to the grid, and your utility gives you a credit on your bill for your share.

Nothing goes on your building. You keep your normal utility account. In most programs you sign up online, and the credit shows up on your bill or on a second bill from the solar company.

How the savings work

Many programs sell you credits at a discount, often in the range of 5% to 15% below what the credits are worth. Here is a worked example:

  • Your bill is $120 a month.
  • Your subscription covers about 80% of your use, so it produces credits worth about $96.
  • You pay the solar company 10% less than that, or about $86.40.
  • Your savings: $96 − $86.40 = $9.60 a month, or about $115 a year.

That is not huge, but it takes almost no effort. Programs exist only in some states and utility areas, and terms change often. Before you sign, check three things: whether there is a sign-up fee, whether there is a cancellation fee, and what happens if you move. Many plans let you move your subscription within the same utility area, or cancel with notice.

Read the contract for the words "guaranteed discount" or "fixed rate." A guaranteed discount moves with your utility's rates, so you always save something. A fixed rate can end up costing more if utility prices drop.

Can you use plug-in or balcony solar panels?

A plug-in solar kit is one to four panels with a small inverter that plugs into a normal outlet. You can put it on a balcony rail, a patio or a small yard. The power it makes feeds the devices running in your home first, which lowers what you buy from the grid.

These kits are common in Germany and spreading elsewhere. Rules for plugging them into a home outlet vary a lot by place and by utility, so read our full plug-in and balcony solar guide before you buy one.

What a small kit can produce

Take an 800-watt kit on a south-facing balcony. In a sunny area it might make about 3.5 kWh on a good day. Over a year, with clouds and shade, a fair estimate is around 800 to 1,000 kWh. At 16 cents per kWh:

  • 900 kWh × $0.16 = $144 a year.
  • If the kit costs $700, the payback is about 700 ÷ 144 = 4.9 years.

Shade, a north-facing balcony or a cheap electricity rate can stretch that out a lot. Try your own numbers in our plug-in solar estimator. The big plus for renters: the kit comes with you when you move.

Is a portable power station with a solar panel worth it?

A portable power station is a big battery with outlets built in. Pair it with a folding solar panel and you can charge it from the sun on a balcony, at a campsite or in a backyard. Brands like EcoFlow, Jackery, Anker and Bluetti all make them.

A power station does not lower your bill much. A 1 kWh unit charged once a day from the sun saves about 365 kWh a year, or about $58 at 16 cents. That is if you use every bit of it, which most people do not.

Where it shines is backup. It can keep a phone, a laptop, a Wi-Fi router, some lights or a CPAP machine running during a power cut. Our power station sizer helps you match the size to what you want to run.

What is a green power plan from your utility?

Many utilities and retail power companies offer a plan where your use is matched with clean energy. They buy renewable energy certificates or sign contracts with wind and solar farms on your behalf.

This option usually does not save money. Some plans cost a little more, often a cent or two per kWh. On 800 kWh a month, a one-cent premium adds $8 to your bill. Some plans in competitive markets cost the same or even less, so it pays to compare.

The point of a green plan is impact, not savings. It is the fastest way to back clean energy as a renter. You can often switch online in a few minutes and switch back if you change your mind.

How do you ask your landlord about solar?

If you rent a house or live in a small building, your landlord might be open to solar. They own the roof, so they control the decision. Your job is to make it easy to say yes.

Landlords care about property value, upkeep and tenant turnover. Solar can help with all three if it is set up well. The tricky part is who pays and who gets the savings. Common setups include:

  • The landlord installs solar and raises rent slightly, while your power bill drops by more.
  • The landlord installs solar on a building where they already pay the electric bill.
  • The landlord allows a small removable system you pay for, such as a plug-in kit.

A short script you can use

Keep it short and friendly. Something like this works well by message or in person:

"Hi, I have been reading about solar and wondered if you had ever looked into it for this property. Solar can raise a home's value and lower power costs. I would be glad to share what I have learned. If a full system is not an option, would you be okay with me putting a small removable solar kit on the balcony? It would not need any drilling or wiring changes."

If they are interested, point them to our quotes page so they can compare installers. Any wiring into the home's electrical panel should be done by a licensed electrician, and you should get any permission in writing.

Do small solar gadgets make a difference?

Solar phone chargers, solar string lights, solar garden lights and small solar fans are cheap and easy. They will not change your bill. A 20-watt panel charging a phone saves maybe a few dollars a year in power.

They still have uses. They help you learn how sun angle and shade affect output. They are handy for camping and outages. And they cost little, often $20 to $80. Just do not expect them to pay you back in money.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get solar tax credits as a renter?

It depends on what you buy and where you live. The US 30% homeowner tax credit (25D) ended for systems installed after Dec 31, 2025. Check current incentives in our incentives guide.

What happens to my community solar plan if I move?

Many plans let you transfer the subscription if you stay in the same utility area. If you leave the area, most let you cancel with notice, but some charge a fee, so read the terms first.

Do I need my landlord's permission for a balcony solar kit?

It is smart to ask, even for a kit that needs no drilling. Many leases cover items on balconies, and some buildings have rules about what you can hang on railings.

Will a green power plan lower my bill?

Usually not. Most green plans cost the same or slightly more. Their main benefit is supporting clean energy, not saving money.
