Selling or buying a home with solar panels

Solar can help or complicate a home sale. Learn how owned, leased and financed systems differ, what documents to gather and what buyers should ask.

By the Simple.solar editorial team. Reviewed October 2026. 6-minute read. How we research.

Short answerAn owned solar system usually helps a home sell, while a lease or loan adds paperwork that needs to be sorted out before closing. Gather your documents early and both sides will have a smoother sale.

More homes come with solar panels every year. That means more sellers asking how solar affects their sale, and more buyers wondering what they are getting into.

The answer depends mostly on one thing: who owns the panels. A system you paid off is simple. A lease or a solar loan can add steps that slow a deal down if no one plans for them. This guide explains the differences, what research says about value and what both sides should check.

Does it matter whether the solar is owned, leased or financed?

Yes, a lot. There are three common setups, and each one changes the sale.

Owned outright

You paid cash or paid off the loan. The panels are part of the house, like a new roof or a furnace. They transfer to the buyer with the home. This is the simplest case and usually the most attractive to buyers.

Financed with a solar loan

You own the panels but still owe money on them. Many solar lenders file a UCC-1 financing statement. This is a public notice that the lender has a claim on the equipment. It shows up in title searches and can slow or stall closing.

Usually you have two choices. You pay off the loan at closing from the sale proceeds, or the lender lets the buyer take over the loan, which is less common. Once the loan is paid, the lender files a release, called a UCC-3. Ask for it in writing and make sure it is recorded.

Leased or on a power purchase agreement (PPA)

A solar company owns the panels. You pay a monthly fee or a price per kWh. When you sell, the buyer must agree to take over the contract, and the solar company must approve them. That usually means a credit check.

If the buyer does not want the lease, your choices are narrower. You may be able to buy out the lease, pay to remove the system, or prepay the remaining payments. Each option costs money, so learn the terms well before you list. The UCC filing on a lease also needs to be handled, though it often stays in place since the company still owns the panels.

Call your solar company or lender before you list your home, not after you get an offer. Lease transfers can take several weeks. Starting early keeps the deal from stalling at the last minute.

Does solar raise home value?

Research says owned solar tends to add value. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) has done several large studies of home sales across many states. In general, they found that buyers paid more for homes with owned solar than for similar homes without it.

A common way to describe the effect is in dollars per watt of system size. Some LBNL work has found premiums in the range of a few dollars per watt. As an example only, if a premium of $3 per watt applied to a 6 kW (6,000-watt) system:

  • 6,000 × $3 = $18,000 in added value.

Real results vary a lot. The premium tends to be larger for newer systems, in places with high power prices and in markets where buyers know solar well. Older systems lose some value as they age, much like a roof does.

Leased systems are a different story. Since the buyer does not get the panels, just the contract, the value effect is usually small or zero. Some buyers see a lease as a negative.

Appraisers do not always know how to value solar. You can help by giving them your production records and system details. Some appraisers use tools built for this purpose, such as models that estimate the value of the future energy the system will produce.

What documents should a seller gather?

Put together a solar folder before you list. A buyer and their agent will want to see it, and an appraiser can use it.

  • The original purchase contract or lease/PPA agreement
  • Loan payoff statement, if you have a solar loan
  • Any UCC-1 filing and, once paid off, the UCC-3 release
  • Panel, inverter and battery warranties, with transfer steps
  • Installer workmanship warranty and roof penetration warranty
  • Permits and final inspection sign-offs
  • Utility interconnection approval (permission to operate)
  • Net metering agreement or rate plan details
  • At least 12 months of production data from your monitoring app
  • 12 months of utility bills showing your savings
  • Login transfer steps for the monitoring app

Some warranties need you to file a form to transfer them to the new owner. Check the fine print, since missing a deadline can void coverage.

What questions should a buyer ask?

If you are buying a home with solar, treat the panels like any big system in the house. Ask these questions early:

  • Who owns the system? Owned, financed or leased? Get the contract.
  • How old is it? Panels often last 25 years or more, but inverters may need replacing around year 10 to 15.
  • How much does it really produce? Ask for at least a year of production data and compare it to the original estimate.
  • Are there any liens or UCC filings? Your title company should find these, but ask directly too.
  • If leased, what are the terms? Monthly payment, yearly price increase (escalator), years left and buyout options.
  • Do the warranties transfer? And what steps are required?
  • How old is the roof under the panels? Removing and reinstalling panels to replace a roof can cost several thousand dollars.
  • What net metering rules apply? Some states let a new owner keep the old rate plan. Others move you to a newer, less generous plan. See our net metering guide and state pages.

A home inspector may not be trained in solar. Consider asking a licensed solar installer to inspect the system, especially if it is older. You can find local installers through our quotes page.

What about tax credits and incentives?

Buyers sometimes ask if they can claim a tax credit on a home that already has solar. In general, credits go to the person who paid for the system when it was installed. The US 30% homeowner tax credit (25D) ended for systems installed after Dec 31, 2025.

Some states and utilities have their own programs, and a few may carry over to a new owner. Check current incentives in our incentives guide, and talk with a tax professional about your own situation.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take my solar panels with me when I move?

Technically yes, but it is rarely worth it. Removal and reinstall costs are high, and your sale contract may count the panels as part of the home. Most sellers leave them.

What if the buyer will not take over my solar lease?

Ask your solar company about a buyout, a prepayment of the remaining term or, as a last resort, removal. Each has a cost, so get quotes before you list.

Does solar make a home sell faster?

Some studies suggest homes with owned solar can sell faster, but results vary by market. Clear paperwork and good production records help more than the panels alone.

What is a UCC-1 filing on solar panels?

It is a public notice that a lender or solar company has a claim on the equipment. It does not usually put a lien on the house itself, but it shows up in title searches and must be dealt with before closing.
